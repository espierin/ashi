CMV–HLA peptide mimicry

Dia 1

Good morning everybody. I would like to start with thanking the ASHI program committee to invite me to the 52nd ASHI meeting to present and discuss our data on how antiviral immunity may interact with recognition of an allogeneic transplant.

Dia 2

Before I move forward, this is my disclosure. [1-2-3-4]

Dia 3

The starting point of my presentation is human cytomegalovirus, or CMV. CMV is an enveloped, double-stranded DNA herpesvirus that remains in the body for life. After transplantation, immunosuppression can allow the virus to reactivate.

But CMV does more than causing infections. It also leaves lasting changes in the immune cells we carry and on what is being recognized. This raises two questions: 1) what happens when CMV is active, and 2) what does the immune system recognize after a previous CMV infection? Because those earlier changes can affect later immune responses, even when the virus is no longer active.

Dia 4

CMV viremia has been associated with poorer rejection-free outcomes, and CMV-related variables also appear among predictors of acute rejection. These associations prompted our question: could a specific immune recognition mechanism connect CMV immunity to graft injury?

Dia 5

The principle that antiviral recognition can include alloreactivity is not new. In this classic study, Burrows and colleagues examined cytotoxic T cells directed against an EBV antigen restricted by HLA-B8. Some of those cells also recognized HLA-B*44:02.

[click]

The response to these B*44 positieve cells was lowered in cold target inhibition assays, adding unlabelled HLA-B8 cells to the assay.

[click]

The important point here is the dual specificity within a T-cell clone. So a clone selected or expanded by an infection can already contain the capacity to respond to an allogeneic HLA target.

Dia 6

This schematic picture isolates that principle. On one hand we have the viral peptide in its restricting self HLA molecule.

[click]

As an alternative we have an allogeneic HLA molecule. The same T-cell receptor can recognize both. The complexes do not need to be identical in sequence, and the response remains dependent on the particular receptor and target. This is a route by which pre-existing antiviral memory could become relevant immediately after transplantation.

[click -> next slides]

The EBV study shows that antiviral T cells can also recognize allo-HLA. Does this also happen with CMV-specific cells?

Dia 10

Amir and colleagues used peptide-HLA tetramers to identify and sort virus-specific T cells. They expanded individual clones, confirmed their viral specificity, and tested them against cells carrying different HLA molecules.

[Click]

Some clones also recognized allo-HLA. Here, a CMV pp50-specific, HLA-A1-restricted clone also recognizes HLA-A11. The same CMV clone clone can therefore mediate antiviral and alloreactive responses. Could this mechanism also matter in patients?

Dia 11

Jasvir Jairam in my lab investigated this clinical potential. Using published CMV-specific cross-reactivity patterns, we identified CMV-seropositive recipients carrying the viral peptide-presenting HLA molecule, paired with donors carrying the reported allo-HLA target. Here, the example is the A1/A11 that I explained before.

[Click]

If this response contributes to graft injury, we would expect more cell-mediated rejection in this group.

[Click]

The comparison group consisted of those with CMV-seronegative recipients and/or absence of a reported cross-reactive HLA combination.

To go short, we found no associations, not even trends, following this stratification. However, certain HLA-C allele groups appeared slightly overrepresented in the rejection group, challenging us to furhter investigations.

Dia 12

When talking about HLA-C and CMV, it is important to note that the CMV UL40 protein encodes a nine-amino-acid sequence highly similar or sometimes even identical to the human HLA class I leader-derived peptide.

[click]

Not all HLA-C leaders contain the identical nonameric peptide; two variants exist. I here listed the HLA-C variants and highlighted the variation at the amino acid level. These nonameric peptides have been reported to be presented by HLA-E, placing a viral product and a host HLA-derived product in a shared immune recognition environment.

Dia 13

The overlap is not restricted to one viral reference sequence. Different CMV UL40 variants resemble different HLA leader peptides, including the LIL, LLL and LVL examples shown here, each associated with with different CMV strains.

Overlap of the peptide sequence depends on the strain and can also involve the HLA-A leader peptide sequence.

Dia 14

In our kidney transplant cohort in Utrecht we observed that, among CMV-seropositive recipients, mismatches involving the LIL and LLL leader peptides were associated with a higher incidence of early cell mediated rejection. Severity distributions were on average the same for the peptide matched and mismatched groups. The LVL comparison did not show these associations.

[click]

An independent case-controle cohort from Sao Paulo, depicted on the right, provided further support for the clinical association; the rejection groups had an overrepresntation of LIL and LLL mismatched tranplant pairs – but not LVL mismatches.

These data support sequence specificity, although they do not by themselves identify the responding cell or prove that UL40-reactive cells caused the rejection.

Dia 15

The time-to-event graph shows the highest early cumulative TCMR in CMV-seropositive recipients with a peptide mismatch, with most events occuring within two weeks after transplantation.

Dia 16

This association remained significant after multivariable adjustment, including PRA and PIRCHE-II scores.

The adjusted hazard ratios were 3.06 for LIL mismatch and 2.61 for LLL mismatch, both statistically significant. CMV seropositivity alone was not significantly associated with TCMR after adjustment.

Dia 17

There is also a recent independent Canadian study being presented tomorrow at this meeting as an oral presenation in the afternoon. I would like to encourage you to attend this presentation to hear more about the details.

Dia 18

To better understand what might be the underlying immunolgy, let me first briefly explain what leader peptides are, how they are processed, and how they become visible to immune cells.

A leader peptide is a short sequence at the beginning of a newly made HLA protein. It directs the protein into the endoplasmic reticulum, where HLA molecules are assembled and loaded with their peptides.

The leader is then cut off by signal peptidase. Further processing by the proteasome generates smaller fragments. A suitable fragment can be transported back into the ER by TAP and loaded into the peptide-binding groove of HLA-E.

The peptide–HLA-E complex then travels to the cell surface, where it can be recognized by NK-cell receptors or specific T-cell receptors. So the leader is removed from the original HLA protein, but part of it can reappear on the cell surface, presented by HLA-E.

Dia 19

Now first consider a recipient with HLA-C*03 and gets confronted with CMV.

[click]

The CMV UL40 peptide in this example is fully identical to the HLA-leader sequence of C*03 in the recipient. And because of this, the viral peptide will be ignored by the immune system of this specific recipient.

Dia 20

Now we have a different recipient with the HLA-C*07:01 as self background. Due to this alternative sefl background, the same viral VMAPRTLIL peptide now differs from self HLA-C peptide.

A potentially responsive repertoire may therefore be generated against the CMV peptide.

[click next slide]

Dia 21

Published studies have identified CD8 T cells that recognize CMV UL40 peptides presented by HLA-E. Because some UL40 peptides are identical to human HLA leader peptides, these cells may also recognize the same peptide when these immune cells are confronted with donor tissue.

Recognition can involve two interacting pathways. The TCR provides peptide-specific recognition, while CD94/NKG2 receptors can modify the response: NKG2C can promote activation, whereas NKG2A can provide inhibition.

The outcome therefore depends on both the peptide–HLA-E complex and the receptors expressed by the responding cell. This offers a possible link between antiviral memory and graft recognition.

Dia 22

If the viral peptide stimulates one or more suitable clones, these clones can differentiate, expand and leave a memory population against this CMV peptide.

Dia 23

The donor now enters the scene with C*03:03 in its HLA. This HLA-C*03:03 contains the LIL leader-derived peptide. This is exactly the same sequence as the viral epitope.

It creates a possible bridge between the recipient's antiviral memory and a peptide generated by the graft. The classical HLA-C molecule is the peptide source. The recognition target proposed in this pathway is this peptide presented by HLA-E.

Dia 24

Now the expanded recipient immune cells encounter the donor tissue. If their receptors recognize the donor's peptide-HLA-E complex, an antiviral response can potentially become a graft-directed response. Indeed, Sullivan and colleagues identified HLA-E-restricted CMV-specific CD8 cells in patients with chronic lung allograft rejection.

[click]

The possible consequence: recognition, cytotoxic effector activity and damage to the graft.

Dia 26

To summarise this part: CMV–HLA-A/-C leader peptide overlap is associated with early TCMR in CMV-seropositive, peptide-mismatched pairs, with adjusted hazard ratios of approximately 2.5 to 3. Independent cohorts and a separate study support the association.

Potential applications are improved risk classification, avoiding relevant VMAPRTLxL mismatches where feasible, adapting immunosuppression, or increasing monitoring. These strategies still require evaluation.

Dia 27

HLA-B introduces a complementary question. Its leader has a methionine/threonine dimorphism at position minus twenty-one, corresponding to position two of the nonameric peptide. Also here, this peptide can influence the HLA-E peptide system.

Dia 28

First, the previous HSCT evidence. Several studies associate donor–recipient HLA-B leader mismatch with acute GVHD, mortality or non-relapse mortality, under both post-transplant cyclophosphamide and ATG-based approaches.

The findings are not uniform: some cohorts show no association or different associations. Yet, the key point is that variation outside the mature HLA-B molecule can carry clinically relevant information in relation to transplant outcomes.

Dia 29

These HSCT findings prompted a different question: does the recipient’s own HLA-B leader genotype also matter in organ transplantation? Following our HLA-A/C studies, we examined MM, MT and TT recipients stratified by their CMV serostatus. Let me start with the kidney data.

Dia 30

We first asked whether CMV status separates outcomes within each recipient genotype. In kidney recipients, the clearest separation was in the M homozygous group, with more early T-cell-mediated rejection in CMV-seropositive patients.

[click]

We do not see the same pattern in TT or MT, although there may be a weak trend for MT recipients. These results point to an effect of the combination of CMV history and leader genotype, rather than an exclusive uniform CMV effect.

Dia 31

We recently examined the same hypothesis in lung transplantation. As in the kidney cohort, the MM genotype was associated with a higher risk of early rejection among CMV seropositive recipients, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 5.77 compared with TT. The MT estimate was also higher, but not statistically significant.

[Click]

Importantly, these patients structurally received substantial T-cell-directed immunosuppression. This does not rule out a T-cell contribution, but it raises the question of whether suppressing parts of the T-cell compartment shifts the balance of the alloimmune response, allowing other immune pathways to become more prominent.

Dia 32

And that brings back the the HSCT setting, where T cell depletion is a common procedure. In our center, we generally apply alpha-beta-depletion and that allowed us to investigate the effect of CMV and HLA-B leader polymorphism in a deplete and replete setting. As shown here, CMV-positive M carriers show less relapse than CMV-negative M carriers and this separation was not present in TT recipients.

[click]

Moreover, in the replete cohort no discrimination based upon CMV and/or HLA-B leaders could be observed at all.

Dia 33

CMV-positive M carriers showed substantially higher NK-cell recovery than CMV-negative M carriers. The dotted lines indicate the upper and lower normal values for healthy individuals.

This separation was not seen in TT recipients.

[click]

The observed differences were statistically significant when evaluated at day three hundred after transplantation.

While the relapse risks seem to coincide with NK cell recovery, this does not provide a causal relation: we still need to establish which population expands and how the affect leukemia controle.

Dia 34

The gamma-delta compartment also differs by leader status. V-delta-two-negative and V-delta-two-positive cells show opposite recovery patterns. So the change is selective: the immune system is being reshaped, rather than every lymphocyte population simply expanding together.

[click]

Bulk TCR sequencing showed a more diverse V-delta-two-positive repertoire in M carriers than in TT recipients. In other words, the response is less dominated by a few large clones. This could reflect more different clones or a more balanced distribution between them. Whether this improves immune recognition or leukemia control is subject to further research.

Dia 35

The relapse outcomes add an important twist. CMV-positive M carriers had fewer relapses overall, but of the twenty-four who did relapse, twenty-one had died in the five-year outcome follow-up time span. The proportion who died after relapse was lower in the other CMV and HLA-B leader groups.

These findings require caution: the groups are small, and this comparison does not account for time since relapse. Still, fewer relapses combined with poor outcomes among those who relapse, raises an important question: could these patients represent a selected group with more resistant disease? The next slide explores two possible explanations.

Dia 36

First, the relevant effector cells may fail to develop or to function effectively, even though the leukemia remains recognizable.

[click]

Alternatively, initial immune control may select for leukemia cells that escape recognition by altering their antigen presentation.

The missing peptide–HLA-E complex illustrates this second hypothesis; we have not demonstrated its loss of target molecules in these patients yet. Comparing paired diagnosis and relapse samples, alongside functional testing of the effector cells, could help distinguish these possibilities.

Dia 37

Together, these findings suggest a shared theme: CMV and HLA-B leader variation may shape immune surveillance, with potentially harmful consequences for a graft and beneficial effects against leukemia. The responsible cells and mechanisms remain to be identified.

Dia 38

Mechanistically and speculatively, peptide-HLA-E recognition could connect these findings. A TCR may provide peptide specificity, while NKG2C promotes activation and NKG2A provides inhibition. CMV can expand NKG2C-positive CD8 memory cells; NK cells may also contribute.

Although leader peptide supply and competition shapes HLA-E expression, more HLA-E does not automatically mean stronger receptor engagement. A better understanding these pathways separately will be essential in order to convert these findings into clinical intervention strategies.

Dia 39

I would like to thank our laboratory, the clinical transplant teams and our collaborators, particularly those who generated these data. And, of course, the patients and donors who made this work possible.

Dia 40

Thank you for your attention. I look forward to answer your questions.
